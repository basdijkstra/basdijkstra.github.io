I've been running [workshops and training courses](/training/) for close to 10 years now, and in that time, I have had the pleasure of working with teams on pretty much every continent, both on technical, hands-on programming courses as well as more strategical workshops. One important lesson I have learned from all these sessions is that

> in-person training is generally much more effective than online courses

There are a number of reasons for this.

### Group work is easier to facilitate

Most of the workshops and training courses I run involve some kind of work to be done in small groups of typically 2-4 people. This could be coming up with testing ideas, reviewing an existing automation code base, pairing up to solve a programming problem, discussing the next steps for a test automation strategy, or something else.

Doing these tasks in small groups improves the quality of the discussions, because people learn not just from me, but also from their peers in the workshop or course. And while most online teaching platforms offer support for working in small groups through break-out rooms, it's simply not the same experience as gathering around a flipover or a screen and discussing the task at hand.

### Engagement is easier to gauge

I pride myself on the ability to adapt my courses and workshops to the particular learning requirements of the group I'm working with. That does not just mean creating a bespoke curriculum through one or more discovery call(s) before the workshop or course, but I also frequently adapt both the content and the pace of the workshop on the fly, based on the feedback of the participants.

That feedback might be explicit, for example when someone asks me to cover a specific question, tool or technique that did not come up in the discovery calls. If time and opportunity allows for it, I'm always happy to do so, and the feedback I get afterwards often reflects that.

Feedback might come to me in a more implicit way, too, though. If I see a large part of the group struggling with the problems at hand, I can slow the pace or clarify things that are unclear. If I see people fiddling with their phones or being otherwise disengaged, I can address that by increasing the pace or introducing additional topics and problems for them to work on.

Both types of feedback, but especially the implicit type, is much harder to get when running an online course. I can't see body language very easily, I can't see if people are doing other things than working on the problems as easily, and that means I can't use that feedback to improve their learning experience.

Sure, I will ask them explicitly if they're happy with the pace and the content several times throughout the day (I make a habit of doing this in all my sessions no matter if it is on-site or online), but experience tells me that people are much more reluctant to tell me where I can improve things in an online session.

### It's easier to help someone out if they're stuck

This one applies specifically to hands-on programming exercises, which make up a significant part of most of my workshops and courses. If someone is stuck, and we're in the same room, I can easily walk over to where they're sitting and point out where the error is if I see it. This could be anywhere from a missing semicolon or closing bracket to changing a sequence of method calls.

Sure, I can do something similar in an online session by asking the participant in question to share their screen, but this is a lot more intrusive to the learning process. There's some technical friction (sharing permissions, people searching for the sharing button and the windows they want to share), but because - at least that's what I assume - everybody hears everything I say, the people who are not a part of the problem-solving conversation still hear me discuss this, which causes a distraction.

In an on-site class, helping someone out can be done without interrupting others. And often, someone sitting next to the person running into the problem can help out, too, freeing up my time and attention to spend on the rest of the group. I always highly appreciate this kind of assistance from people in the audience.

### I get better questions and conversations

Finally, the quality of the questions and conversations in an on-site workshop or course will often be a lot better, too. Sure, I've had very lively online training sessions (those are great fun), as well as in-person training sessions where people simply didn't ask a lot of questions (those are hard work). Generally speaking, though, the engagement and conversations will be significantly more lively when people are in the same room.

And that, in turn, often contributes positively to the quality of the course itself as well as to how participants experience it. When conversation is flowing, people will more easily ask questions, or bring up examples from their own context. Often, this will lead the conversation from 'how does this tool or technique work?' to 'where and how can we use this in our own context?', and I can't tell you how valuable discussing that during the workshop is.

Again, I'm not saying that discussions like these are not happening in my online courses, and I will do everything I can to keep the conversation going in every session I facilitate, but experience once again tells me that in-person sessions will generally see more of these conversations.

So, in short, I strongly prefer on-site training over online. Of course, there will always be situations where gathering all participants into the same room isn't practical, and for those clients, I am more than happy to run the training sessions from the comfort of my own office.

When given the choice, I will always choose coming into your office, though, for all the reasons listed in this post. It will not cost you a lot more, as my rates for on-site and online training are very similar, and the added value of everybody being in the same room will often pay for the additional investment many times over.
